# DSAR, Full Worked Case

**Setting:** a mid-sized financial-services firm (retail banking and lending).
**The request:** *"I want a copy of all the personal data you hold about me."*
**Requester (fictional):** an existing customer, referenced here as the data subject.

---

## Step 1, Intake and logging

The request arrives by email to customer services and is forwarded to the privacy team.

**Actions:**
- Log the request with a reference number and the **date received** (the one-month clock under Article 12(3) starts now).
- Record the channel, the requester's stated identity, and what they have asked for.
- Acknowledge receipt to the requester.

**Key point:** a valid access request does not have to use the words "DSAR" or cite the GDPR. *"Send me my data"* is a valid request. Staff are trained to recognise and forward these, because a missed request becomes a missed deadline.

**Timeframe:** one month from receipt, extendable by two further months for complex or numerous requests, provided the requester is told of the extension and why within the first month.

---

## Step 2, Identity verification

Before releasing any personal data, the firm must be satisfied of the requester's identity, otherwise it risks disclosing one person's data to another.

**Actions:**
- Verify identity **proportionately**, using information the firm already holds, rather than demanding excessive new documentation.
- For a known customer, confirmation through the authenticated banking channel is stronger and less intrusive than asking for ID copies by email.

**Key point:** identity checks must be proportionate. Over-demanding documentation is itself a way of frustrating the right of access, and regulators treat it as such. The clock is generally paused only where verification is genuinely needed, not used as a delaying tactic.

---

## Step 3, Locating the data

This is the operationally hard part. Personal data about one customer sits across multiple systems:

| System | Likely personal data |
|--------|----------------------|
| Core banking platform | Account details, transactions, balances |
| Lending / credit system | Applications, credit decisions, repayment history |
| CRM | Contact details, service notes, relationship history |
| Marketing platform | Preferences, consents, campaign history |
| Call-centre / recordings | Call logs, and possibly recorded calls |
| Email / correspondence | Letters and emails to and from the customer |

**Actions:**
- Use the data map to query each system for the customer's records.
- Include **unstructured** data (emails, call recordings, notes), which is where firms most often fall short.
- Assemble the results in one place for review.

**Key point:** you can only find what you have mapped. A current data inventory (see Project 1) is what makes a DSAR answerable at all. Unstructured and legacy data is the usual gap.

---

## Step 4, Exemptions and third-party data

Not everything located is automatically disclosed. Each item is reviewed.

**Considerations:**
- **Other people's data.** If a record contains a third party's personal data (for example another individual named in a call note), that person's data is redacted or withheld unless it is reasonable to disclose. The right of access does not entitle the requester to others' data (Art. 15(4), rights and freedoms of others).
- **Legal privilege / ongoing investigations.** Certain material may be subject to specific exemptions under national law implementing the GDPR (for example legal professional privilege, or fraud-prevention and financial-crime exemptions relevant in a financial-services context).
- **Confidential business information** is not a blanket exemption; only recognised grounds justify withholding.

**Actions:**
- Review each item, redact third-party data, and document the reason for any redaction or withholding.

**Key point:** the exemptions analysis is where judgement matters most. Withholding must be justified and recorded, not assumed. Over-redaction fails the right of access; under-redaction breaches others' rights.

---

## Step 5, Response package

**What the response must include (Article 15):**
- A **copy of the personal data** undergoing processing.
- The **purposes** of processing.
- The **categories** of personal data.
- The **recipients** or categories of recipient.
- The **retention period** (or the criteria for it).
- The existence of the **rights** to rectification, erasure, restriction, and objection, and the right to complain to the supervisory authority.
- The **source** of the data, where not collected from the individual.
- Whether there is **automated decision-making**, and meaningful information about the logic (relevant in a lending context, see Project 2).

**Actions:**
- Provide the data in a **commonly used electronic format** where the request came electronically.
- Deliver securely (for example through the authenticated banking channel, not unprotected email).
- Include a plain-language covering note explaining what is enclosed and the customer's rights.

**Sample covering line:** *"Enclosed is a copy of the personal data we hold about you, along with information about how and why we use it and the rights available to you. Some information has been redacted where it relates to other individuals."*

---

## Step 6, Audit trail and close

**Recorded for accountability (Art. 5(2)):**
- Date received and date responded (proof the deadline was met).
- Identity-verification method.
- Systems searched.
- Exemptions or redactions applied, with reasons.
- What was disclosed and how it was delivered.

**Close** the request on the register, retaining the record as evidence.

**Key point:** the audit trail is what turns a one-off response into a defensible process. If a complaint reaches the Ombudsman, this record is the firm's evidence that the request was handled correctly.

---

## Where DSARs usually go wrong (reflection)

- **Missed intake.** A request in plain language is not recognised, and the clock runs down before anyone acts.
- **Unstructured data.** Emails, notes, and call recordings are forgotten, so the response is incomplete.
- **Identity checks used as a delay.** Excessive documentation demands frustrate the right and draw regulator criticism.
- **Blanket redaction.** Over-withholding on vague "confidentiality" grounds rather than a recognised exemption.
- **No audit trail.** The response goes out but nothing is recorded, so the firm cannot later prove it complied.

Handling a DSAR well is less about the deadline and more about **process, thoroughness, and evidence**, which is exactly what this case study demonstrates.
